The idea to keep with you

System design is not memorizing buzzword diagrams; it is quantifying bottlenecks first, choosing explicit consistency and latency trade-offs, isolating fault domains, and keeping the architecture as simple as the workload allows.

From patterns to decisions.

Learn to design distributed systems from first principles—quantifying traffic and storage bounds, modeling data and APIs, and reasoning rigorously about partitioning, caching, consensus, and disk storage.

  • ↗Translate product requirements into QPS, bandwidth, memory, and 5-year storage bounds.
  • ↗Design idempotent APIs, choose REST/gRPC/async protocols, and protect services with rate limiting and backpressure.
  • ↗Model relational schemas, B-Tree composite indexes, and ACID isolation levels without lock contention.
  • ↗Select NoSQL families, design shard keys to avoid hot partitions, and generate distributed 64-bit IDs.

01
System Design / Stage 01

Fundamentals: Foundations.

Every sound design begins by narrowing functional scope to core user journeys and quantifying non-functional constraints: read/write QPS (average and peak 3x-10x), p99 latency budgets, availability SLAs (99.9% vs 99.99%), durability (RPO/RTO), and 5-year storage growth. Back-of-the-envelope math reveals whether a workload fits comfortably on a single vertical server with read replicas or genuinely demands horizontal sharding and multi-region replication. Premature distribution adds network hops, consensus overhead, and operational failure modes without solving a measured bottleneck.

Back-of-the-Envelope Capacity Bounds

Convert DAU and request rates into QPS (1M req/day ≈ 12 QPS; 100k QPS needs horizontal scale), ingress/egress Mbps, 5-year disk bytes, and 20% hot working-set RAM.

Availability, Durability, and Consistency

Distinguish uptime SLAs (99.99% allows 52 minutes/year downtime) from durability (RPO=0 requires synchronous replication across failure domains) and consistency latency.

Order platform / Right-Sizing an Internal Analytics Portal Before Over-Engineering

Right-Sizing an Internal Analytics Portal Before Over-Engineering.

A team proposed Kafka, Cassandra, and a 12-microservice mesh for an internal dashboard serving 50,000 daily employees.

Capacity math showed 50k DAU * 20 requests/day = ~12 average QPS (60 peak QPS) and 40 GB/year of append data—well within a single managed PostgreSQL primary with one read replica and connection pooling.

Lesson: Quantify QPS, working-set memory, and growth first; a single vertical node with a replica beats a premature distributed cluster.
Pause & reason: where would you draw the boundary?▼
  • What are the average and peak read/write QPS, and how asymmetric is the read-to-write ratio?
  • How much of the hot working set fits in RAM versus requiring SSD/NVMe IOPS?
  • What are the concrete p99 latency, availability (SLA), and data-loss (RPO) requirements?
  • Can a single well-indexed primary plus read replicas satisfy the next 24 months of growth?

02
System Design / Stage 02

Fundamentals: APIs, Services and Protocols.

Service boundaries depend on explicit contracts and transport choices. Public APIs favor HTTP/REST or GraphQL over TLS with cursor-based pagination and Idempotency-Key headers for safe retries, while internal East-West calls favor gRPC over HTTP/2 with strongly typed Protobuf schemas and binary framing. Transport matters at scale: TCP suffers head-of-line blocking under packet loss, which HTTP/3 over QUIC eliminates via independent UDP streams. At the ingress boundary, L4 load balancers route TCP connections at line rate while L7 proxies inspect paths, terminate TLS, and enforce token-bucket rate limiting, timeouts, and priority load shedding.

HTTP/REST + JSONExposing public external APIs, browser clients, or third-party webhooks.Cursor pagination (avoid OFFSET at scale)., Idempotency-Key headers on POST., Additive backward-compatible schemas.
gRPC + Protobuf (HTTP/2)High-throughput internal microservice-to-microservice RPCs or bidirectional streaming.Strict deadlines propagated per hop., Field-number stability in .proto files., L7-aware load balancing (not sticky TCP).
Async Message / Event StreamDecoupling slow fanout, notifications, or bursty writes from synchronous user latency.Bounded consumer lag & DLQs., At-least-once idempotent consumers., Backpressure on producers.

L4 vs L7 Load Balancing & HTTP/2 Pinning

Because gRPC multiplexes many RPCs over one long-lived HTTP/2 TCP connection, an L4 load balancer will pin all traffic to one backend pod; use L7 request-level balancing.

Overload Protection: Little's Law & Load Shedding

By Little's Law (L = λW), rising downstream latency multiplies in-flight concurrency. Enforce hard concurrency caps, token-bucket rate limits, and priority load shedding before queues OOM.

Order platform / Fixing Skewed gRPC Traffic Behind an L4 Load Balancer

Fixing Skewed gRPC Traffic Behind an L4 Load Balancer.

After scaling a recommendation gRPC service from 4 to 20 pods during a traffic spike, CPU stayed at 95% on the original 4 pods while the 16 new pods sat idle.

Clients held persistent HTTP/2 connections routed through an AWS NLB (L4), so new pods never received existing streams. Switching to Envoy L7 gRPC load balancing with max connection age rotated streams evenly across all 20 pods.

Lesson: Multiplexed HTTP/2 and gRPC protocols require L7 request-level load balancing or periodic connection cycling to distribute load.
Pause & reason: where would you draw the boundary?▼
  • Which endpoints require synchronous confirmation versus asynchronous queue ingestion?
  • How are mutating APIs made idempotent when mobile clients retry timed-out requests?
  • Does your load balancer balance at L4 connections or L7 requests for multiplexed HTTP/2/gRPC traffic?
  • What happens to caller thread pools if a downstream service's p99 latency jumps 10x?

03
System Design / Stage 03

Fundamentals: Data Modeling and SQL.

Relational databases (PostgreSQL, MySQL/InnoDB) excel when workloads need ACID transactions, foreign-key integrity, and flexible queries. Performance hinges on B-Tree index design: placing high-selectivity equality columns before range columns in composite indexes, using covering indexes (INCLUDE) to enable Index-Only Scans, and avoiding leading-wildcard or non-sargable predicates. Under concurrency, Multi-Version Concurrency Control (MVCC) lets readers avoid blocking writers, while isolation levels (Read Committed, Repeatable Read/Snapshot, Serializable/SSI) govern anomalies like lost updates and write skew. Scale SQL via read replicas (accounting for replication lag), connection poolers (PgBouncer), and online non-blocking migrations.

Composite B-Tree Column Ordering (Equality -> Sort -> Range)

In an index on (tenant_id, status, created_at), equality filters on tenant_id and status narrow the B-Tree slice first so created_at range scans remain contiguous.

Preventing Lost Updates & Write Skew

Read Committed does not prevent lost updates on read-modify-write cycles; use atomic UPDATE ... WHERE, SELECT ... FOR UPDATE, or optimistic version checks.

Order platform / Eliminating Double-Booking Under Snapshot Isolation

Eliminating Double-Booking Under Snapshot Isolation.

A meeting-room scheduler running PostgreSQL Repeatable Read allowed two concurrent requests to book the same room for overlapping times because each transaction queried for conflicts before inserting.

Snapshot Isolation prevents dirty and non-repeatable reads, not write skew across disjoint rows. Adding a PostgreSQL EXCLUDE USING gist constraint on (room_id, tsrange) or upgrading to Serializable (SSI) made overlapping inserts mutually exclusive at the database engine level.

Lesson: Repeatable Read / Snapshot Isolation does not prevent write skew; enforce multi-row invariants with explicit locks, exclusion constraints, or SSI.
  • Adding a standard B-Tree index without CONCURRENTLY locks table writes in production.
  • Every additional secondary index accelerates reads at the cost of extra B-Tree page writes and VACUUM/undo overhead.
Pause & reason: where would you draw the boundary?▼
  • Do your composite index column orders match your query's equality, sort, and range predicates?
  • Which read-modify-write workflows are vulnerable to lost updates or write skew under Read Committed or Snapshot Isolation?
  • How does your application handle read-your-own-writes when routing reads to asynchronous replicas?
  • Can your schema migrations run online without acquiring ACCESS EXCLUSIVE table locks?

04
System Design / Stage 04

Fundamentals: NoSQL, Partitioning and IDs.

When write throughput or dataset size outgrows a single primary, systems adopt horizontal partitioning and NoSQL engines tailored to specific access patterns: key-value (Redis, DynamoDB), document (MongoDB), wide-column (Cassandra, ScyllaDB), or graph (Neo4j). Hash partitioning spreads keys uniformly via consistent hashing with virtual nodes, minimizing key movement during node scaling, but sacrifices efficient range scans. Range partitioning supports ordered scans but risks hot partitions on monotonic keys (like timestamps). Distributed ID generators—such as 64-bit Snowflake IDs (timestamp + worker ID + sequence) or UUIDv7—avoid central auto-increment bottlenecks while preserving time-ordered B-Tree/LSM locality.

ChoiceWhat it buysWhat it costs
Hash Partitioning (Consistent Hashing)Distributes writes evenly across shards. Virtual nodes limit rebalance movement to ~1/N keys when nodes join or leave.Range queries must scatter-gather across all shards.
Range PartitioningFast sequential range scans within a single shard.Monotonically increasing keys (dates, auto-IDs) create severe hot-shard write bottlenecks.

Mitigating Hot Partition Keys

When a celebrity user or flash-sale SKU saturates one shard, append a bounded random salt suffix (e.g., key#0..9) on writes and scatter-read across the 10 sub-keys.

Time-Ordered 64-Bit IDs (Snowflake / UUIDv7)

Embed millisecond timestamps in the high bits so inserts append to the right edge of B-Tree/LSM indexes instead of causing random page splits, while keeping worker IDs unique to prevent collisions.

Order platform / Solving a Hot-Shard Outage in a 10M DAU URL Shortener & Analytics Store

Solving a Hot-Shard Outage in a 10M DAU URL Shortener & Analytics Store.

A URL shortener partitioned click-analytics in Cassandra by (short_code, day). When a viral campaign link hit 40,000 clicks/sec, a single Cassandra partition grew past 500 MB and timed out.

Engineering changed the partition key to (short_code, day, bucket_0_to_15) and switched short-URL generation to 64-bit Snowflake IDs encoded in Base62. Write load spread evenly across 16 partitions with zero central DB sequence locks.

Lesson: Partition keys must bound both write rate per key and maximum partition size over time.
Pause & reason: where would you draw the boundary?▼
  • Does your access pattern require cross-entity joins and ACID transactions (SQL), or key/partition-scoped high write throughput (NoSQL)?
  • What happens to your shard distribution if one tenant or celebrity key receives 100x average traffic?
  • How do you handle clock backward-drift in a Snowflake ID generator?
  • Are your secondary indexes local to each shard or global across shards (requiring scatter-gather or async updates)?

05
System Design / Stage 05

Fundamentals: Caching and Fast Reads.

Caching absorbs read-heavy traffic in memory (sub-millisecond Redis/Memcached or microsecond in-process L1 caches) and at edge CDNs. Cache-Aside loads lazily on miss and tolerates cache outages gracefully, while Write-Through keeps cache and DB synchronous at higher write latency, and Write-Behind batches writes asynchronously at the risk of data loss if a cache node crashes. Production caches fail in predictable ways: Cache Stampedes (thundering herds when a hot key expires), Cache Penetration (repeated queries for non-existent keys bypassing the cache), and Cache Avalanches (mass simultaneous TTL expiration). Mitigate these with singleflight/SETNX request coalescing, Bloom filters or short-TTL negative caching, and randomized TTL jitter.

Stampede, Penetration, and Avalanche Defenses

Use singleflight/mutex coalescing so only one caller recomputes a missed hot key; add TTL jitter (±15%) to prevent synchronized expiry; cache empty/null results briefly or check a Bloom filter to stop penetration.

Eviction Policies: LRU, LFU, and TinyLFU

LRU (hash map + doubly linked list) is simple but vulnerable to one-time scan pollution; LFU/W-TinyLFU retains genuinely hot items by tracking access frequency.

Order platform / Stopping a Midnight Cache Avalanche on an E-Commerce Homepage

Stopping a Midnight Cache Avalanche on an E-Commerce Homepage.

Catalog items were cached in Redis with a fixed 3600-second TTL populated by a nightly batch job. Every hour on the hour, thousands of keys expired simultaneously, sending a 30x query spike that crashed PostgreSQL.

The team added ±20% random TTL jitter, wrapped hot category lookups in per-process singleflight coalescing plus Redis probabilistic early recomputation, and subscribed to Debezium CDC events for targeted invalidation.

Lesson: Never assign identical static TTLs to batch-loaded cache keys; combine TTL jitter with miss coalescing.
  • In-process L1 caches are faster than Redis but create per-pod staleness; keep L1 TTLs very short (seconds) or invalidate via pub/sub.
  • Always size your database for at least partial cache-miss bursts so a cold Redis restart does not trigger a permanent outage.
Pause & reason: where would you draw the boundary?▼
  • What happens to your primary database if the entire Redis cluster is flushed or restarted cold?
  • How do you prevent 5,000 concurrent requests for an expired hot key from all hitting the database at once?
  • What is the maximum acceptable staleness window for each cached entity?
  • How are non-existent key lookups (cache penetration) prevented from bypassing the cache?

06
System Design / Stage 06

Fundamentals: Distributed Coordination.

Distributed nodes share no memory and cannot rely on physical wall clocks, which drift continuously. Under CAP and PACELC, a network partition forces a choice between linearizable Consistency (CP) and Availability (AP), while normal operation still trades Latency (L) for Consistency (C). Consensus protocols like Raft and Paxos elect a single leader per term via majority quorums (floor(N/2) + 1) and replicate a deterministic log. Even with distributed locks (etcd, ZooKeeper, Redis), a client paused by Garbage Collection can wake up after its lease expires and issue stale writes—making monotonically increasing fencing tokens validated by the storage layer mandatory for correctness.

Fencing Tokens Against Stale Leaders (GC Pauses)

Every lock acquisition returns a monotonically increasing token (e.g., 33, 34). Storage nodes reject any write carrying a token lower than the highest token already seen, neutralizing zombie leaders.

Quorum Intersection (R + W > N) & Logical Clocks

In Dynamo-style quorum stores, choosing R + W > N ensures read and write quorums overlap, while Lamport/Vector clocks or Hybrid Logical Clocks (HLC) detect causal ordering and concurrent conflicts without synchronized NTP.

Order platform / Preventing Split-Brain File Corruption with Fencing Tokens

Preventing Split-Brain File Corruption with Fencing Tokens.

Two worker nodes coordinated exclusive writes to an object store using a 10-second Redis lease. Worker A experienced a 15-second JVM GC pause; Worker B acquired the lease, and when Worker A resumed, it overwrote Worker B's state.

The team moved lock coordination to etcd with revision-based fencing tokens and added an optimistic compare-and-swap (If-Match / version >= token) check on the storage engine. Worker A's stale write was rejected immediately.

Lesson: A distributed lock without storage-enforced fencing tokens is only an advisory optimization, never a mutual-exclusion guarantee.
  • Quorum intersection (R + W > N) alone does not guarantee linearizability without read-repair or leader coordination.
  • Never use NTP wall-clock timestamps for last-write-wins (LWW) conflict resolution on critical financial or inventory state.
Pause & reason: where would you draw the boundary?▼
  • During a network partition, should this service reject writes (CP) or accept potentially divergent writes (AP)?
  • If a lock holder freezes for 30 seconds in a GC pause, what stops it from corrupting state when it resumes?
  • How does your system order concurrent events across regions without trusting physical server clocks?
  • What is the write latency impact of synchronous majority quorum replication across availability zones?

07
System Design / Stage 07

Fundamentals: Storage Engines.

Storage engines bridge in-memory data structures and durable NVMe/SSD blocks. B-Trees (PostgreSQL, InnoDB) update fixed-size 4KB-16KB pages in place, delivering predictable low-latency point and range reads at the cost of random page writes and write amplification. Log-Structured Merge-Trees (RocksDB, Cassandra) append writes sequentially to a Write-Ahead Log (WAL) and an in-memory MemTable (skiplist), flushing immutable sorted SSTables to disk. Reads in LSM-Trees check the MemTable and multiple SSTable levels using Bloom filters to skip non-matching files, while background compaction merges SSTables to reclaim deleted/overwritten space. Cloud object stores (S3) separate metadata partition maps from erasure-coded immutable byte chunks.

ChoiceWhat it buysWhat it costs
B-Tree Storage Engine (OLTP / SQL)Predictable, fast point lookups and ordered range scans (single tree traversal). No background compaction stalls.In-place random page writes and WAL full-page writes increase write amplification. Page fragmentation wastes disk space over time.
LSM-Tree Storage Engine (Write-Heavy / NoSQL)High sequential write throughput via MemTable + append-only WAL. Higher compression ratios on immutable sorted SSTables.Read amplification across multiple SSTable levels (mitigated by Bloom filters). Background compaction can cause I/O contention and tail-latency write stalls.

The RUM Conjecture (Read, Update, Memory/Space)

Optimizing any two of Read overhead, Update/write overhead, and Memory/space amplification worsens the third; choose B-Trees for read-heavy OLTP and LSM-Trees for write-intensive ingestion.

Write-Ahead Log (WAL) Durability

Every mutation is appended and fsynced to the sequential WAL before acknowledging the client, enabling crash recovery of un-flushed MemTable or dirty buffer-pool pages.

Order platform / Resolving Periodic Write Stalls in a RocksDB Telemetry Pipeline

Resolving Periodic Write Stalls in a RocksDB Telemetry Pipeline.

An observability ingestion cluster backed by RocksDB suffered 5-second p99 write stalls every 10 minutes even though average disk utilization was under 50%.

Telemetry showed L0 SSTables piling up faster than background compaction could merge them into L1, triggering RocksDB's write-stall backpressure. Increasing background compaction threads, tuning L0 slowdown triggers, and switching from size-tiered to leveled compaction on dedicated NVMe bandwidth eliminated the stalls.

Lesson: LSM-Tree write throughput is ultimately bounded by background compaction capacity, not just raw MemTable append speed.
Pause & reason: where would you draw the boundary?▼
  • Is your workload constrained by random point-read latency (favoring B-Trees) or sustained high-rate writes (favoring LSM-Trees)?
  • How many SSTable levels might a missing key traverse if Bloom filters are disabled or undersized?
  • What is your WAL fsync policy—per-commit (RPO=0) or group-commit/periodic (higher throughput, bounded loss window)?
  • How do you isolate background compaction or vacuum I/O so it does not starve foreground user queries?

The decision framework

01

What do the QPS, working-set RAM, and 5-year storage numbers demand?

Let capacity math—not hype—decide whether a vertical primary + replica suffices or sharding is required.

02

Which paths need synchronous ACID guarantees vs asynchronous durability?

Decouple hot user paths from slow fanout, indexing, and analytics pipelines.

03

What is the partition key, and how does it behave under 100x hot-key skew?

Poor shard keys create single-node bottlenecks that horizontal scaling cannot fix.

04

How do caches and storage engines behave under misses, compaction, and partitions?

Design for stampedes, CAP/PACELC trade-offs, fencing tokens, and background compaction I/O.

05

What fails gracefully when a tier slows down or disconnects?

Enforce L7 load balancing, deadlines, idempotent retries, and bounded load shedding at every hop.

What to take away

Good architecture is a set of justified decisions.

Strong system design starts with capacity math and clear contracts, selects SQL or NoSQL and B-Tree or LSM storage to match real access patterns, protects hot paths with caching and async decoupling, and enforces correctness under partitions with quorums, fencing tokens, and bounded overload controls.
