This walkthrough focuses on The Real-Time Median Problem: Why Sorting the Whole Stream Fails.

Before making a design choice, clarify:

  • Spotify processes millions of salary records and wants to display the median compensation in real time as each new record arrives.
  • The naive approach — collect all records, sort them, pick the middle — has two fatal flaws: sorting is O(N log N) and you would have to re-sort after every single insertion.
  • The Two Heaps pattern eliminates this waste by keeping the data permanently organized around a single invariant: the entire lower half of the data set lives in one heap, and the entire upper half lives in another.

Concepts in play: tree-data-structures, jvm-internals-and-garbage-collection, core-algorithm-patterns, database-indexing

Stage 1 — Max-Heap and Min-Heap: Partitioning a Stream into Two Halves

  • A max-heap always exposes its largest element at the top.
  • A min-heap always exposes its smallest element at the top.
  • These two properties are exactly what you need to see the boundary between "smaller than median" and "larger than median" without scanning the collection.
  • Here is the core insight spelled out plainly: - Put every number less than or equal to the current median in the max-heap .

Applies tree-data-structures to the decision above.

import java.util.Collections;
import java.util.PriorityQueue;

// Max-heap: lower half of the stream
PriorityQueue<Integer> lowerHalf = new PriorityQueue<>(Collections.reverseOrder());

// Min-heap: upper half of the stream
PriorityQueue<Integer> upperHalf = new PriorityQueue<>();

Observable outcome: Here is the core insight spelled out plainly: - Put every number less than or equal to the current median in the max-heap .

Stage 2 — The Balancing Rule: Keeping the Two Heaps in Sync

  • After every insertion, the heap sizes must satisfy this invariant: lowerHalf.size() - upperHalf.size() is always 0 or 1.
  • When both heaps have equal size, the median is the average of their tops.
  • When the lower half has one extra element, the median is lowerHalf.peek() .
  • Push the new number into lowerHalf (max-heap).

Stage 3 — Deep Dive: How Java's PriorityQueue Implements the Two Halves

  • Internals: PriorityQueue Memory Layout and the Max-Heap Trick Java's PriorityQueue is a binary heap stored in a flat array .
  • For a min-heap of size N, the element at index i is always less than or equal to both children at indices 2i+1 and 2i+2 .
  • The root (index 0) is always the minimum — peek() returns it in O(1) by simply reading queue[0] .
  • offer(x) appends the new element at the end of the array and then sifts up : it swaps the element with its parent repeatedly until the heap property is satisfied.

Stage 5 — Real-World Uses: Salary Analytics, Sliding Windows, and Capital Allocation

  • The Two Heaps pattern solves a surprisingly wide class of problems because "median of a growing or sliding data set" appears in many systems: Streaming analytics platforms — Compensation analysis tools at Spotify, Linke…
  • Re-sorting the entire list after each update is impractical at millions of records.
  • Two Heaps reduces each update to O(log N) regardless of total size.

  • The median is always at one or both tops.
