A foundational engineering concept

Metrics Cardinality
Control.

Metrics cardinality is the number of unique label-value combinations (metric_name + {k=v}) tracked in a Time-Series Database; unbounded labels like user_id or request_id explode inverted indexes, exhausting TSDB RAM and crashing Prometheus/Mimir.

THE BIG IDEA / Key takeaway · CONCEPT GUIDE

Never put high-cardinality identifiers (user IDs, IPs, URLs with UUIDs, pod hashes) into metric labels; keep active series bounded by dropping/normalizing labels at scrape time and use Logs or Trace Exemplars for per-request debugging.

Explanation anchor
01 / The idea

Foundational mechanics & core principles

In Prometheus, VictoriaMetrics, and Mimir, every unique combination of metric name and label values creates a separate time series with its own in-memory Head chunk, inverted index entry, and 2-hour block compaction cost. A single developer adding user_id or raw http_path to a counter or 12-bucket histogram can turn 500 series into 50 million series overnight—consuming 150+ GB of RAM, stalling compaction, and spiking Datadog/cloud monitoring bills by 10x.

  • Total cardinality is the Cartesian product of active label values: 50 endpoints × 5 status_codes × 4 methods × 100 pods × 12 histogram buckets = 1,200,000 series from one metric.
  • In Prometheus, each active series consumes roughly 3–5 KB of RAM in the Head block; 10 million active series requires ~30–50 GB of RAM before any query runs.
  • Normalize dynamic URLs (/users/98421/orders -> /users/:id/orders) inside the HTTP router middleware before incrementing Prometheus/OTel counters.
  • Use OpenTelemetry attributesprocessor or Prometheus metric_relabel_configs (labeldrop, drop, keep) to strip runaway labels at ingestion.
  • Exemplars attach a trace_id to a histogram bucket sample without creating a new time series, giving you one-click drilldown from a p99 latency spike to the exact user/request trace.

Good candidates

  • Instrumenting multi-tenant or high-traffic microservices with Prometheus, OpenTelemetry, or Datadog where metrics aggregate across thousands of pods and endpoints.
  • Enforcing guardrails at the OTel Collector or Prometheus scrape/remote-write layer before untrusted application metrics hit central TSDB storage.
  • Designing histograms and SLOs (http_request_duration_seconds) where multiplying N labels by 12–15 le buckets rapidly multiplies total series count.

Think twice

  • When you genuinely need per-user, per-request, or per-IP debugging—use structured logs (Loki/ClickHouse), distributed traces (Tempo/Jaeger), or Exemplars instead of metrics.
  • Analytics/BI pipelines (BigQuery, ClickHouse, Snowflake) designed for high-cardinality GROUP BY user_id queries rather than 15-second TSDB scrape intervals.
  • Tiny single-node deployments (<10k total series) where aggressive label-dropping rules add operational complexity without meaningful memory savings.
Deep Dive & Production Considerations
02 / Concrete walkthrough

Architectural mechanics & implementation

ScenarioA new Spring Boot microservice exposed http_server_requests_seconds using the raw request URI (/api/v1/invoices/inv_88219a) instead of the matched route template. Within 4 hours, active series jumped from 180k to 14.2M.

The idea

Time-Series Databases (TSDBs) like Prometheus, VictoriaMetrics, and Mimir are optimized for low-cardinality numerical aggregation over time, not arbitrary relational indexing. Every unique combination of metric name and key-value labels (e.g., http_requests_total{method="GET", route="/users/:id", status="200"}) is assigned a unique internal Series ID.

While individual numerical samples compress remarkably well (~1.37 bytes per sample using Gorilla XOR and delta-of-delta encoding), the inverted index mapping every label_name=label_value pair to its list of Series IDs—plus the active Head block holding the last 2 hours of chunks—lives in RAM. Cardinality control is the discipline of keeping that Cartesian product bounded so memory, disk index size, and query fan-out stay predictable.

Concrete walkthrough

Trace how a single misconfigured metric is caught and neutralized before it can OOM-kill a shared Prometheus/Mimir cluster:

  1. Application emission: An instrumented API emits http_request_duration_seconds with 12 le buckets, route="/v1/orders/ord_99123", user_id="u_4410", and status="200". Across 50,000 active users in 2 hours, this produces 50,000 × 12 = 600,000 active series from one endpoint.
  2. OTel Collector / Scrape guardrail: Before ingestion, the OTel Collector attributesprocessor (or Prometheus relabel_configs) inspects the metric. It strips user_id, normalizes route via regex/router template to /v1/orders/:id, and moves trace_id into an Exemplar attachment rather than a label.
  3. Quota enforcement: Prometheus enforces sample_limit: 25000 and label_limit: 25 on the scrape job. If an unapproved metric still breaches the cap, the scrape fails with a sample_limit exceeded alert rather than poisoning the Head block.
  4. TSDB Head & Compaction: The normalized metric creates just 12 series in the TSDB Head block (~48 KB RAM instead of ~2.4 GB). Every 2 hours, the Head block flushes cleanly to an immutable disk block and ships to S3/GCS via Thanos/Mimir.

Normalizing labels at the app/collector boundary keeps the TSDB Head block bounded while Exemplars preserve trace-level drilldown.

Designing cardinality budgets and alternatives

Treat metric cardinality like a finite memory budget across your organization:

  • Rule of thumb: Keep any single metric under 10,000–50,000 active series, and keep label_values per label under 100 wherever possible.
  • Exemplars for high-cardinality context: Instead of labeling http_request_duration_seconds with tenant_id or order_id, enable OpenMetrics Exemplars. Prometheus stores only the latest exemplar per series/bucket in a tiny ring buffer, linking latency outliers directly to Jaeger/Tempo traces.
  • Native Histograms: Classic histograms multiply cardinality by N le buckets (typically 10–15). Prometheus Native Histograms pack exponential buckets into a single series, immediately cutting histogram cardinality by 90%.

Failure modes

1. Silent target drop from sample_limit: Setting sample_limit protects Prometheus, but if nobody alerts on up == 0 or scrape_samples_post_metric_relabeling > limit, a service loses all observability silently after a deploy.

2. Label explosion from error messages: Putting error="connection refused to 10.0.4.18:5432" in a metric label creates a new series for every transient IP/port/timeout string. Always map errors to a fixed enum (error_type="db_timeout").

3. Multi-tenant Mimir/Cortex ingestion rejection: Shared TSDBs enforce max_global_series_per_user (e.g., 150,000). A single runaway team can hit the tenant cap and drop all production alerts for that tenant.

Auditing cardinality in production

Use Prometheus's built-in TSDB status endpoints and PromQL diagnostics to catch leaks before they page you:

  • TSDB Top 10: Query /api/v1/status/tsdb (or topk(10, count by (__name__)({__name__=~".+"}))) to rank metrics by active series count.
  • Churn rate: Alert on rate(prometheus_tsdb_head_series_created_total[15m])—steady creation of thousands of new series per minute indicates ephemeral labels (pod_uid, request_id) leaking into metrics.
  • Head memory: Track prometheus_tsdb_head_series * 4096 as a quick lower bound on Head RAM requirements.

Practical checklist

  • Enforce route template normalization (/users/:id, never /users/12345) in all HTTP/gRPC server middleware.
  • Ban user_id, session_id, ip, trace_id, and raw exception strings from metric labels in code review and CI linters.
  • Set sample_limit and label_limit on every Prometheus scrape job and max_global_series_per_user on multi-tenant remote-write.
  • Replace 12+ bucket classic histograms with Native Histograms (or reduce buckets to 6–8 critical SLO thresholds).
  • Alert on up == 0 and rate(prometheus_tsdb_head_series_created_total[15m]) spikes to catch cardinality regressions immediately after deploys.
03 / Engineering trade-offs

Weighing architectural decisions

Coarse Metric Labels vs. Granular Per-Pod / Per-Tenant Labels

Advantage: Dropping `pod` or `tenant_id` from high-frequency histograms slashes TSDB RAM and query latency by 10–100x.

Drawback: You lose the ability to slice a p99 latency spike by a specific noisy neighbor tenant or faulty pod directly in Grafana.

Ingestion-Time `metric_relabel_configs` vs. App-Level Instrumentation

Advantage: Collector/Prometheus relabeling acts as a central circuit breaker that protects the TSDB even when a team deploys a buggy metric.

Drawback: Dropping a label via `labeldrop` without pre-aggregating creates duplicate timestamp collisions (`out of order` / `duplicate sample` errors) unless you use `metricstransform` aggregation.

Native Histograms vs. Classic `le` Bucket Histograms

Advantage: Native (exponential) histograms store all buckets inside a single time series, cutting histogram cardinality by 10–20x while eliminating manual bucket tuning.

Drawback: Requires Prometheus >= 2.40+ / OTel Collector support and PromQL `histogram_quantile()` syntax adjustments across existing dashboards.

04 / Failure modes & resilience

Explain where it breaks · Known failure modes

Head-Block OOM & Compaction Stall

Rapidly churning ephemeral labels (e.g., `pod_name` or `job_id` in short-lived CronJobs) fill the Prometheus 2-hour Head block with millions of dead series; compaction exceeds memory limits and crashes in a loop.

What helps: Set `sample_limit: 50000` and `label_limit: 30` per scrape job so rogue targets fail to scrape rather than crashing the central TSDB.

Duplicate Sample Collisions After `labeldrop`

Using `labeldrop` in Prometheus to strip `instance` or `user_id` leaves multiple distinct series with identical remaining labels; Prometheus rejects the colliding timestamps and drops data.

What helps: Drop the entire offending metric (`action: drop`) at scrape time or aggregate it properly using OTel Collector `metricstransform` / recording rules before storage.

Inverted Index Query Timeout

Queries like `sum by (route) (rate(http_requests_total{env="prod"}[5m]))` must intersect postings lists across millions of series, causing 30s+ query timeouts and Grafana 504s.

What helps: Pre-aggregate high-fanout metrics using Prometheus Recording Rules (`job:http_requests:rate5m`) and limit regex matchers (`=~ ".*"`) on high-cardinality labels.

Churn from Ephemeral Batch / CI Jobs

Pushgateway or short-lived serverless functions emit metrics with unique `execution_id` or `commit_sha` labels, creating permanent series churn that bloats the TSDB block index for 15–90 days.

What helps: Never include `execution_id` or `timestamp` in labels; emit batch outcomes as structured log events or aggregate via StatsD/OTel Delta temporality.

05 / Practical checklist

Avoid these mistakes

  • Adding `user_id`, `email`, `ip_address`, `trace_id`, or `request_id` as a Prometheus/OTel metric attribute.
  • Logging raw HTTP paths (`/orders/12345`) or unparameterized SQL queries as metric labels instead of route templates (`/orders/:id`).
  • Creating a 20-bucket histogram with 5+ labels (`method`, `route`, `status`, `pod`, `az`, `tenant`) without calculating the Cartesian product.
  • Setting `sample_limit: 0` (unlimited) on Kubernetes service-discovery scrape configs, allowing any developer deploy to take down shared monitoring.
  • Using `labeldrop` in `metric_relabel_configs` without realizing it causes timestamp collisions instead of summing the dropped dimension.
