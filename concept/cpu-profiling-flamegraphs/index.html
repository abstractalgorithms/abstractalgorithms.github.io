THE BIG IDEA / Key takeaway · CONCEPT GUIDE

Flame Graphs measure inclusive time on the horizontal axis—not chronological time—so wide plateaus reveal where your CPU actually spends cycles, even when scattered across thousands of calls.

Explanation anchor
01 / The idea

Foundational mechanics & core principles

Instrumenting every function entry/exit distorts timings by 10x–100x (the observer effect) and misses kernel/libc time. Statistical sampling profilers like Linux perf, async-profiler (JVM), pprof (Go), and py-spy (Python) inspect instruction pointers at 99–999 Hz with <2% overhead in production. Visualizing millions of raw stack traces as a Flame Graph turns unreadable megabytes of text into an instant structural map of CPU bottlenecks, lock contention, and unexpected allocations.

  • Sampling at a prime-adjacent frequency like 99 Hz or 997 Hz prevents lockstep aliasing with periodic 10 ms or 100 Hz application timers.
  • The X-axis of a Flame Graph is sorted alphabetically by frame name (not chronological time); the width of each box represents the percentage of total samples containing that frame.
  • The Y-axis represents call-stack depth (bottom = root/entry point, top = leaf function currently executing on CPU); wide flat tops ('plateaus') indicate self-time hotspots.
  • Missing frame pointers (-fomit-frame-pointer in C/C++/Rust) break fast kernel stack unwinding unless DWARF unwinding (--call-graph dwarf, slow) or ORC/SFrame tables are used.
  • Safepoint bias in traditional JVM profilers skews samples to GC safepoints; tools like async-profiler use AsyncGetCallTrace + perf_events to capture true async stacks across native and Java frames.

Good candidates

  • Diagnosing high CPU utilization, tail-latency spikes (p99), or throughput plateaus in production services with <2% overhead.
  • Comparing before/after performance of a refactor using Differential Flame Graphs (red = regression, blue = speedup).
  • Uncovering hidden runtime tax such as JVM safepoint bias, Go GC mark/sweep overhead, JSON serialization, or kernel syscall time.

Think twice

  • Debugging threads blocked on network I/O, disk reads, or mutexes—on-CPU sampling ignores sleeping threads; use off-CPU profiling (offcputime eBPF) or distributed tracing instead.
  • Measuring single-digit nanosecond microbenchmarks where sampling period (1–10 ms) is 1,000,000x coarser than the function; use hardware PMU counters (perf stat) or criterion/JMH.
  • Tracing rare, single-request logic bugs that occur once in a million requests; statistical sampling will likely miss the event.

02 / Concrete walkthrough

Architectural mechanics & implementation

ScenarioA Go API gateway hit 85% CPU at 12k RPS. Running go tool pprof -http=:8080 http://pod:6060/debug/pprof/profile?seconds=30 generated a Flame Graph.

The idea

CPU profiling answers one question: which code paths are consuming CPU cycles? Instrumenting every function call adds prohibitive overhead and skews short functions. Instead, modern profilers use statistical sampling: a kernel hardware timer or PMU (Performance Monitoring Unit) interrupts each CPU core at a fixed rate (e.g., 99 times per second), records the currently executing instruction pointer and its call stack, and resumes execution.

Raw stack samples are noisy—thousands of identical stacks repeated in arbitrary order. Brendan Gregg's Flame Graph solves this by folding identical call stacks together, summing their sample counts, sorting sibling frames alphabetically on the X-axis, and plotting stack depth on the Y-axis. The width of any rectangle is directly proportional to the fraction of CPU time spent in that function and its descendants.

Concrete walkthrough

Suppose we profile a Rust/C++ service on Linux with perf for 30 seconds at 99 Hz:

  1. Capture: perf record -F 99 -a -g --call-graph fp -- sleep 30
    • -F 99 avoids 100 Hz timer aliasing.
    • -g --call-graph fp walks the RBP frame-pointer chain in kernel space (or use dwarf if frame pointers are omitted).
  2. Collapse: perf script | stackcollapse-perf.pl > out.folded
    • Converts raw addresses + symbols into single-line semicolon-delimited stacks with counts, e.g.: main;handle_req;parse_json;serde_json::from_slice 1420 main;handle_req;verify_sig;ed25519_verify 890
  3. Render: flamegraph.pl out.folded > cpu.svg
    • Total samples = 2,310. main and handle_req span 100% width at the bottom.
    • parse_json spans 61.5% (1420/2310) on the left; verify_sig spans 38.5% on the right.
    • At the top of parse_json, serde_json::from_slice forms a flat plateau—confirming JSON parsing is the primary self-time hotspot.

End-to-end sampling pipeline from kernel interrupt to interactive Flame Graph visualization.

Reading & interpreting flame graphs

When reading a Flame Graph, ignore colors (in standard Flame Graphs, warm colors are randomized for contrast; only in Differential or eBPF/JIT graphs do colors encode kernel vs. user space or regression vs. speedup).

Look for two shapes:

  • Wide plateaus (flat tops): Functions at the top of a wide stack with nothing above them. These are self-time hotspots—tight loops, hashing, compression, or serialization.
  • Wide towers with many thin spires: A parent function is wide, but its children split into many narrow calls. This indicates high inclusive time caused by calling a helper too often (e.g., logging, locking, or small allocations inside a loop).

Always check the bottom-most kernel frames if system CPU (%sys) is high: frames like entry_SYSCALL_64 -> sys_futex or tcp_sendmsg indicate lock contention or unbuffered small writes.

Failure modes

  1. Container permission denial: Docker/Kubernetes drops CAP_PERFMON and CAP_SYS_PTRACE by default, blocking perf_event_open() unless configured via seccomp or privileged sidecars.
  2. Inlined functions hiding bottlenecks: Aggressive -O3 inlining collapses hot helpers into their callers; enable DWARF inline info (-g) so the symbolizer can reconstruct virtual inline frames.
  3. Missing JIT symbol maps: Profiling JVM or V8 without --perf-basic-prof or async-profiler leaves 80% of the Flame Graph as hexadecimal addresses.
  4. Ignoring off-CPU latency: A service with 10% CPU and 500 ms p99 latency is waiting on I/O, DNS, or mutexes—an on-CPU Flame Graph will only show the 10% active work and mislead the investigation.

Continuous production profiling

Ad-hoc profiling fails when incidents are transient. Continuous profilers (Parca, Pyroscope, Google Cloud Profiler, Datadog Continuous Profiler) run eBPF or runtime profilers as a DaemonSet across every node at ~19–99 Hz, uploading collapsed profiles every 10–60 seconds.

This enables Differential Flame Graphs between canary and baseline deployments (e.g., v2.4.1 vs v2.4.0): frames that grew wider turn red and frames that shrank turn blue, catching 5% CPU regressions before full rollout.

Practical checklist

  • Build native binaries with -fno-omit-frame-pointer and split debug symbols (-g).
  • Sample at a non-divisor frequency (99 Hz or 997 Hz) under realistic load.
  • Verify perf_event_paranoid <= 1 or grant CAP_PERFMON in container specs for eBPF/perf.
  • Check %usr vs %sys before profiling; include kernel frames if %sys exceeds 15%.
  • Use Differential Flame Graphs to validate optimizations rather than eyeballing widths.
  • Switch to off-CPU (offcputime) or mutex contention profiling whenever CPU is low but tail latency is high.
03 / Engineering trade-offs

Weighing architectural decisions

Sampling Frequency (99 Hz vs. 999+ Hz)

Advantage: Higher frequencies (999 Hz) capture short-lived bursts and reduce variance in shorter 10-second profiles.

Drawback: Increases interrupt/unwinding overhead, ring-buffer drops, and profile file size on high-core-count machines.

Frame Pointer (`fp`) vs. DWARF Stack Unwinding

Advantage: Frame-pointer unwinding (`-fno-omit-frame-pointer`) walks stacks in ~100 ns inside the kernel with tiny ring-buffer records.

Drawback: DWARF unwinding (`perf record --call-graph dwarf`) copies 8 KB of raw stack per sample, causing 5%–15% overhead and disk I/O spikes, though it works on stripped binaries.

On-CPU Sampling vs. Off-CPU Tracing

Advantage: On-CPU sampling has near-zero overhead because it only fires when a core is actively executing instructions.

Drawback: Completely blind to lock contention, `epoll_wait`, and disk stalls; off-CPU eBPF tracing instruments every context switch, which adds overhead under high scheduler churn.

04 / Failure modes & resilience

Explain where it breaks · Known failure modes

Broken Stacks (`[unknown]` Frames)

Compiled binaries built with `-fomit-frame-pointer` or stripped JIT symbols produce single-frame towers labeled `[unknown]`.

What helps: Compile production C/C++/Rust services with `-fno-omit-frame-pointer` (now default in Fedora/Ubuntu and <1% cost on x86_64), keep debuginfo packages, and generate `/tmp/perf-<pid>.map` for JIT runtimes.

Lockstep Sampling Aliasing

Sampling at exactly 100 Hz (10 ms) synchronizes with a 10 ms ticker or cron loop, causing that task to appear as 0% or 100% of CPU.

What helps: Always use non-divisor frequencies such as 99 Hz, 499 Hz, or 997 Hz.

JVM Safepoint Bias

Profilers relying on JVMTI `GetAllStackTraces` only sample threads when they reach a JVM safepoint, blaming allocation-heavy or loop-exit lines instead of tight compute loops.

What helps: Use `async-profiler` or JFR with `AsyncGetCallTrace` + Linux `perf_events` rather than safepoint-based samplers.

Misreading X-Axis as Wall-Clock Time

Engineers assume left-to-right frames executed sequentially in time and chase narrow transient spikes.

What helps: Read bottom-to-top for call hierarchy and look strictly for wide horizontal plateaus (high self-time) or wide subtrees (high inclusive time).

05 / Practical checklist

Avoid these mistakes

  • Profiling an idle or under-loaded staging instance where 90% of samples sit in `idle` or `epoll_wait`, hiding real lock and cache bottlenecks.
  • Forgetting that Flame Graphs normalize to 100% of *captured samples*, not 100% of machine capacity—a 50% bar at 5% total CPU is only 2.5% of a core.
  • Leaving `perf.data` un-collapsed on production hosts until it fills `/tmp` (999 Hz DWARF can write >50 MB/s).
  • Looking only at self-time (`top` view) and missing a death-by-a-thousand-cuts caller allocating objects across 20 tiny helper functions.
  • Comparing two Flame Graphs visually by eye instead of generating a Differential Flame Graph (`difffolded.pl`).
