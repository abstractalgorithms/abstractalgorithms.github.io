Two-Phase
Commit.

Two-Phase Commit (2PC) coordinates an atomic all-or-nothing transaction across independent shards or services by splitting execution into a durable PREPARE voting round followed by a COMMIT or ABORT decision.

THE BIG IDEA / Key takeaway · CONCEPT GUIDE

2PC guarantees atomicity across distributed participants, but a coordinator crash during the `PREPARE` window leaves participants blocked holding locks until the coordinator recovers.

Explanation anchor
01 / The idea

Foundational mechanics & core principles

Distributed databases (Google Spanner, CockroachDB, Citus) and XA transaction managers use 2PC whenever a single business operation must atomically update records living on different shards or resource managers without partial writes.

  • Phase 1 (Prepare): The coordinator sends PREPARE to all participants; each participant acquires locks, writes undo/redo records to its local WAL, fsyncs to disk, and replies YES (or NO).
  • Phase 2 (Commit/Abort): Only if every participant votes YES does the coordinator durably log COMMIT to its own WAL and send COMMIT to all participants; any NO or timeout triggers ABORT.
  • Once a participant logs PREPARED and votes YES, it surrenders unilateral autonomy—it cannot abort or commit on its own and must hold locks until the coordinator decides.
  • 2PC is a blocking protocol: if the coordinator crashes after participants vote YES (and before sending COMMIT/ABORT), participants remain stuck in-doubt until the coordinator restarts.
  • Modern NewSQL systems (Spanner, CockroachDB, TiDB) eliminate single-node coordinator blocking by replicating both the coordinator state and each shard via Paxos/Raft.

Good candidates

  • Cross-shard ACID transactions inside a single distributed database (e.g., transferring funds between Shard A and Shard B in Spanner or Citus) where participants are backed by Raft/Paxos.
  • Legacy enterprise integrations requiring XA-compliant PREPARE/COMMIT across a relational DB and a message broker (IBM MQ, ActiveMQ) in low-throughput back-office flows.
  • Short-lived (<50 ms) distributed state transitions inside a single datacenter with high-availability replicated coordinators.

Think twice

  • Cross-microservice workflows spanning independent teams, external payment gateways (Stripe), or third-party APIs; use the Saga pattern or Transactional Outbox + CDC instead.
  • Geo-distributed multi-region writes with high WAN RTT (100–200 ms) unless latency is acceptable or parallel-commit optimizations (CockroachDB) are used.
  • High-throughput event pipelines (>50k TPS) where synchronous fsync round-trips and cross-node lock holding collapse concurrency.
Deep Dive & Production Considerations
02 / Concrete walkthrough

Architectural mechanics & implementation

ScenarioA banking core splits accounts across two PostgreSQL shards (shard_a and shard_b) and executes a $500 transfer using PostgreSQL PREPARE TRANSACTION / COMMIT PREPARED.

The idea

When data lives on two independent nodes (say, Shard A and Shard B), a local ACID commit on Shard A cannot be rolled back if Shard B crashes a millisecond later. Two-Phase Commit (2PC) solves this by introducing a Coordinator and splitting commitment into two durable steps:

  1. Phase 1 (Voting / Prepare): Can every participant guarantee it can commit?
  2. Phase 2 (Decision / Commit): Now that every participant has durably promised, execute the commit (or abort if anyone said no).

The core invariant is irrevocability: once a participant writes PREPARED to its Write-Ahead Log and votes YES, it cannot unilaterally roll back—even if it reboots.

Concrete walkthrough

Walk through transferring $100 from Account 1 (Shard A) to Account 2 (Shard B) under transaction tx_42:

  1. Phase 1 (PREPARE): The coordinator sends PREPARE tx_42 to Shard A and Shard B. Shard A checks balance >= 100, acquires an exclusive row lock on Account 1, writes the redo/undo log plus PREPARE tx_42 to disk (fsync), and replies VOTE_YES. Shard B locks Account 2, fsyncs PREPARE tx_42, and replies VOTE_YES.
  2. Coordinator Decision: Having received VOTE_YES from all participants, the coordinator writes COMMIT tx_42 to its own WAL (fsync). At this exact instant, the transaction is logically committed.
  3. Phase 2 (COMMIT): The coordinator sends COMMIT tx_42 to Shard A and Shard B. Each participant writes COMMIT to its WAL, releases the row locks on Account 1 and Account 2, and sends ACK back so the coordinator can garbage-collect tx_42.

Every participant must fsync its PREPARE record before voting YES; once the coordinator logs COMMIT, Phase 2 retries until all participants acknowledge.

Why vanilla 2PC blocks

Consider what happens if the coordinator crashes right after Shard A and Shard B vote YES, but before it sends COMMIT (or ABORT).

Shard A cannot abort tx_42 because the coordinator might have logged COMMIT and already told Shard B to commit. Conversely, Shard A cannot commit because the coordinator might have crashed before logging COMMIT and will abort on recovery. Therefore, Shard A is blocked in the PREPARED state, holding exclusive locks on Account 1 until the coordinator recovers (or an administrator intervenes). Note that participant-to-participant queries cannot safely resolve this if all participants are uncertain.

Failure modes

In production, 2PC failures center on lock duration and orphaned prepared state:

  • Prepared transaction leaks: If a client/coordinator disconnects permanently, prepared transactions linger in pg_prepared_xacts (PostgreSQL) or InnoDB XA RECOVER, holding locks and blocking garbage collection (VACUUM).
  • Cascading timeout storms: A slow disk on one shard causes Phase 1 to stall; meanwhile, incoming transactions queue behind the locked rows held by PREPARED transactions, exhausting connection pools across all shards.

How modern systems make 2PC fast

Modern distributed databases retain 2PC's strong isolation while fixing its latency and availability flaws:

  1. Consensus-backed Participants & Coordinator: Every shard and the transaction record itself are Raft/Paxos groups, eliminating single-point-of-failure blocking.
  2. Parallel Commits (CockroachDB): Instead of waiting sequentially for PREPARE to finish before writing COMMIT, CockroachDB writes a STAGING transaction record in parallel with intent writes, cutting commit latency from 2 consensus RTTs down to 1 RTT.
  3. Read-Only Optimization: Participants that only read data (no writes) skip Phase 2 entirely by replying READ_ONLY in Phase 1.

Practical checklist

  • Never use 2PC across external third-party APIs or independent microservice boundaries; use Outbox + idempotent consumers or Sagas.
  • If using PostgreSQL XA (PREPARE TRANSACTION), set alerts on pg_prepared_xacts count and age (>30s) to prevent VACUUM stalls.
  • Replicate the coordinator state (via Raft/Paxos or a HA database) so a single machine failure never leaves locks stranded.
  • Keep 2PC transactions short and bounded—never perform network RPCs to external services or user prompts between PREPARE and COMMIT.
  • Enforce idempotent Phase 2 COMMIT/ABORT retries with exponential backoff until every participant acknowledges.
03 / Engineering trade-offs

Weighing architectural decisions

2PC vs. Saga (Compensating Transactions)

Advantage: 2PC provides strict serializable isolation—no intermediate uncommitted state is ever visible to concurrent readers.

Drawback: 2PC holds locks across network round-trips and blocks on coordinator failure, whereas Sagas release locks immediately and scale across external APIs.

2PC vs. Transactional Outbox + CDC

Advantage: 2PC updates two systems synchronously in one request-response cycle.

Drawback: Outbox + Debezium avoids distributed locks entirely, delivering higher throughput and availability at the cost of eventual consistency.

Vanilla 2PC vs. Presumed-Abort 2PC

Advantage: Presumed-Abort skips synchronous `fsync` of abort records on the coordinator, saving disk I/O on failed transactions.

Drawback: Requires participants to treat missing coordinator state as an abort, adding protocol state-machine subtleties.

04 / Failure modes & resilience

Explain where it breaks · Known failure modes

Coordinator Crash Leaving In-Doubt Locks

The coordinator dies after participants flush `PREPARED` to WAL. Participants hold row/table locks indefinitely, piling up blocked queries across the cluster.

What helps: Replicate the coordinator state via Raft/Paxos, or set strict `max_prepared_transactions` monitoring with runbooks for manual heuristic resolution only as a last resort.

Network Partition & Heuristic Commit Split-Brain

An operator manually runs `COMMIT PREPARED` on Shard A during a partition while the coordinator later decides `ABORT` on Shard B, corrupting cross-shard invariants.

What helps: Never mix automatic 2PC with manual heuristic overrides unless the entire transaction outcome is verified across all participants.

Tail-Latency Amplification (Straggler Participant)

A single slow participant (GC pause, noisy-neighbor disk `fsync`) delays Phase 1 `PREPARE`, forcing every other healthy participant to hold locks longer.

What helps: Enforce aggressive Phase 1 prepare timeouts (e.g., 200–500 ms) so stalled transactions abort quickly and release locks.

Forgotten Prepared Transactions Exhausting Slots

Orphaned `PREPARE TRANSACTION` entries in PostgreSQL hold back the `xmin` horizon, blocking `VACUUM` and causing severe table bloat.

What helps: Alert on `pg_prepared_xacts` age > 60 seconds and enforce automated coordinator reaper jobs.

05 / Practical checklist

Avoid these mistakes

  • Using XA/2PC across HTTP microservices over the public internet instead of idempotent Sagas or Outbox patterns.
  • Omitting `fsync` on the participant's `PREPARED` WAL record—if a participant crashes and forgets its `YES` vote after the coordinator commits, atomicity is broken.
  • Confusing 2PC (an atomic commit protocol) with Raft/Paxos (a consensus protocol for replicating a single state machine); 2PC requires consensus on the coordinator to be non-blocking.
  • Assuming 3-Phase Commit (3PC) solves all 2PC blocking in production—3PC still fails under network partitions (FLP impossibility) and is rarely used.
  • Ignoring `max_prepared_transactions` limits in PostgreSQL (default is `0`, disabling 2PC).
