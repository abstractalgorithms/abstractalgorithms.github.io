Encryption at Rest
& in Transit.

Encryption in transit protects data moving across untrusted networks via TLS 1.3/mTLS, while encryption at rest protects persisted disks and backups using AES-256-GCM envelope encryption backed by a KMS.

The idea to keep with you

When a service delegates authority across hops, terminate TLS 1.3 everywhere (including internal service-to-service mTLS), use envelope encryption (DEK + KMS KEK) for storage, and separate key-management permissions from data-storage permissions.

What are Encryption in Transit & Envelope Encryption at Rest.

Why this matters: The mechanism: understand how Encryption in Transit & Envelope Encryption at Rest works from first principles.

Data faces two distinct threat surfaces:

  • In Transit: TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446) uses 1-RTT ephemeral ECDHE (Perfect Forward Secrecy) + AEAD ciphers (TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384). Inside clusters, mTLS authenticates both client and server via short-lived X.509 certificates (Istio/SPIRE).
  • At Rest: Envelope encryption encrypts payloads locally with a fast symmetric Data Encryption Key (DEK) (AES-256-GCM + random 96-bit nonce + AAD), then wraps the DEK with a hardware-backed KMS Key Encryption Key (KEK).

Engineering Decision Framework
Problem
How to implement Encryption at Rest & in Transit without hitting unbounded tail latency, state divergence, or operational fragility.
Naive Solution
Use a single unpartitioned path with unbounded retries and synchronous global coordination.
Why It Fails
Global coordination serializes throughput under load, while unbounded retries amplify transient stalls into full outages.
Alternatives
Compare synchronous strict-consistency paths against bounded asynchronous replication, local caching, or partitioned ownership.
Engineering Decision
When a service delegates authority across hops, terminate TLS 1.3 everywhere (including internal service-to-service mTLS), use envelope encryption (DEK + KMS KEK) for storage, and separate key-management permissions from data-storage permissions.
Trade-Offs
Gains predictable p99 latency and fault isolation in exchange for explicit bounding, observability, and slightly higher implementation complexity.

STAGE 04 · HOW IT IS MANAGEDL5 · APPLIED DESIGN

How to Prevent GCM Nonce Reuse, Bind AAD & Cache DEKs.

Why this matters: Operational control: configure bounds, failure handling, and safe production defaults.

1.

Never Reuse a 96-Bit GCM Nonce: Reusing (key, nonce) leaks the XOR of plaintexts and compromises GHASH—always generate os.urandom(12) per call and rotate DEKs well before 2^30 messages. 2. Bind AAD (tenantId:recordId): Stops an attacker with DB write access from copying Tenant A's ciphertext into Tenant B's row. 3.

How to Prevent GCM Nonce Reuse, Bind AAD & Cache DEKs

# AES-256-GCM authenticated encryption with fresh 96-bit nonce & tenant AAD (Python)
import os
from cryptography.hazmat.primitives.ciphers.aead import AESGCM

def encrypt_field(dek_32b: bytes, plaintext: bytes, tenant_id: str, record_id: str) -> tuple[bytes, bytes]:
    nonce = os.urandom(12)  # Fresh 96-bit CSPRNG nonce per encryption
    aad = f"{tenant_id}:{record_id}".encode("utf-8")
    ciphertext = AESGCM(dek_32b).encrypt(nonce, plaintext, aad)
    return nonce, ciphertext
