A foundational engineering concept

SLOs, SLAs &
Error Budgets.

SLOs set internal reliability targets (e.g., 99.95% availability), SLAs attach legal or financial penalties to external commitments (e.g., 99.9%), and error budgets quantify the exact allowance of bad requests or downtime (1 - SLO) before feature velocity must pause for reliability work.

THE BIG IDEA / Key takeaway · CONCEPT GUIDE

Set your internal SLO tighter than your external SLA so your error budget triggers engineering intervention before customer credits or contract breaches happen.

Explanation anchor
01 / The idea

Foundational mechanics & core principles

Without quantified reliability targets, product teams push risky releases until an outage forces a reactive freeze, or ops teams block all changes in pursuit of an unaffordable 100% uptime. A 99.9% monthly SLO allows 43.8 minutes of downtime (or 100,000 failed requests out of 100M), turning reliability from an emotional argument between Dev and Ops into a shared, measurable budget.

  • SLI (Service Level Indicator) is the raw ratio: good events divided by valid total events (e.g., HTTP responses with status < 500 and latency < 300ms).
  • SLO (Service Level Objective) is the target percentage over a rolling or calendar window (e.g., 99.95% over 30 days = 21.9 minutes of allowed downtime).
  • Error Budget = 100% - SLO; when remaining budget hits zero, feature deploys freeze and engineering shifts to reliability fixes.
  • SLAs (Service Level Agreements) are external contracts with financial remedies (e.g., 10% service credit below 99.9%) and must always be looser than internal SLOs.
  • Multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerting (e.g., 14.4x burn over 1h AND 5m) catches fast outages in minutes while ignoring brief, harmless spikes.

Good candidates

  • Operating customer-facing APIs, payment gateways, or SaaS platforms where reliability and feature velocity directly compete.
  • Enforcing automated deployment gates (e.g., Argo Rollouts, Spinnaker) and multi-window burn-rate alerts in Prometheus or OpenTelemetry.
  • Negotiating realistic tier guarantees with enterprise customers by backing external SLAs with stricter internal SLOs.

Think twice

  • Early-stage prototypes or pre-product-market-fit MVPs with a handful of test users where instrumentation overhead outweighs signal.
  • Offline, best-effort batch scripts or internal ad-hoc tooling with no downstream consumers or latency expectations.
  • Low-traffic endpoints (<10 requests/minute) where a single failed health check or transient blip immediately blows a short-window error budget.
Deep Dive & Production Considerations
02 / Concrete walkthrough

Architectural mechanics & implementation

ScenarioA payments service handles 50M requests/month with a 99.95% availability SLO (25,000 allowed 5xx errors). A buggy release pushes 5xx rate to 1.2% during a 10% canary.

The idea

Every production system faces a permanent tension between shipping speed and stability. SLIs, SLOs, and Error Budgets replace subjective arguments with a shared mathematical contract:

  • SLI (Service Level Indicator): A carefully defined quantitative measure of service behavior, almost always expressed as a ratio: Good Events / Valid Events (excluding 4xx client errors or health-check noise). Example: proportion of /v1/orders HTTP requests returning < 500 within 250ms.
  • SLO (Service Level Objective): The target reliability threshold for an SLI over a measurement window (typically 28 or 30 rolling days). Example: 99.95% over 30 days.
  • Error Budget: The inverse of the SLO (100% - SLO). For a 99.95% SLO over 30 days (43,200 minutes), the budget is 0.05%—exactly 21.6 minutes of total outage or 50,000 failed requests per 100M requests.
  • SLA (Service Level Agreement): A legal contract with customers specifying consequences (service credits, refunds, termination rights) if reliability falls below a looser threshold (e.g., 99.9%).

Instead of treating every error as a failure, an error budget treats reliability as a spendable asset. If the budget is healthy, teams ship fast and run chaos experiments. If the budget is exhausted, non-emergency feature releases pause until reliability work restores margin.

Concrete walkthrough

Consider an Order Checkout API serving 2,000 req/s (~5.184 billion requests per 30-day rolling window) with a 99.95% availability + latency SLO (status < 500 and duration <= 300ms).

  1. Budget calculation: 0.05% * 5.184B = 2,592,000 allowed bad requests per 30 days (or 86,400 bad requests/day at steady load).
  2. Burn rate definition: A burn rate of 1x consumes 100% of the 30-day budget in exactly 30 days (0.05% error rate). A burn rate of 14.4x (0.72% error rate) consumes 2% of the monthly budget in 1 hour and would exhaust the entire month in 50 hours.
  3. Multi-window alerting (Sloth / Prometheus): At 14:00 UTC, a database connection-pool leak pushes the bad-request ratio to 0.9% (18x burn rate).
    • Both the 1-hour window and 5-minute short window exceed 14.4x burn rate by 14:06 UTC.
    • Alertmanager pages the primary on-call engineer via PagerDuty (Severity: Page).
  4. Automated mitigation & budget accounting: At 14:14 UTC, the engineer rolls back the bad deployment. Total incident duration is 14 minutes at 18x burn, consuming 14m * 18 / 43,200m = 0.58% of the monthly budget.
  5. Reset prevention: Because the 5-minute window drops back below 14.4x immediately after rollback, the page auto-resolves without flapping, leaving 99.42% of the monthly budget intact.

SLIs feed multi-window burn-rate evaluators that drive both human paging and automated deployment gates.

SLI math and multi-window burn rates

Alerting on raw error percentages fails because a 1% error rate is catastrophic for a 99.99% service (100x burn) but normal for a 98% batch API (0.5x burn). Burn rate normalizes alerts to the SLO:

Burn Rate = Observed Error Ratio / (1 - SLO)

Google SRE's standard multi-window pairing prevents both slow detection and slow reset:

  • Page (2% budget in 1h): 1h burn >= 14.4x AND 5m burn >= 14.4x. Detects a 100% outage in ~2 minutes and resolves within 5 minutes of a fix.
  • Page (5% budget in 6h): 6h burn >= 6x AND 30m burn >= 6x. Catches moderate, sustained degradation (e.g., 0.3% errors on a 99.95% SLO).
  • Ticket (10% budget in 3d): 3d burn >= 1x AND 6h burn >= 1x. Files a Jira/Linear ticket for slow leaks without waking anyone up at 3 AM.

Failure modes

  • Dependency Chain Multiplication: If your API synchronously calls three downstream services each with a 99.9% SLO, your combined ceiling is 0.999^3 = 99.7% unless you add fallbacks, caches, or async queues.
  • Reset Lag on Single-Window Alerts: Alerting on a single 1-hour window keeps firing for up to 55 minutes after an outage is fixed; always pair with a short window (1/12th of the long window).
  • Metric Cardinality Explosion: Adding user_id or request_id labels to Prometheus SLI histograms crashes TSDB memory. Restrict SLI labels to service, route, method, and status_class.
  • Silent Client-Side Failures: Measuring SLIs only inside the origin pod misses DNS failures, TLS handshake drops, and CDN/ALB 502s. Measure at the edge or via synthetic probes.

Error budget policy in practice

An error budget only works when paired with a pre-agreed escalation policy signed off by Engineering and Product before an incident occurs:

  1. Budget > 25% remaining: Normal feature velocity; teams may run controlled fault-injection tests.
  2. Budget 1%–25% remaining: Require peer review on high-risk config/schema changes; prioritize top reliability debt items in the next sprint.
  3. Budget <= 0% (Exhausted): Freeze all non-reliability feature deployments; route 100% of engineering capacity to P0/P1 postmortem actions, automated rollbacks, or dependency circuit breakers until the rolling window recovers.
  4. Single-Incident Cap: If one catastrophic incident consumes >20% of the monthly budget, mandate a blameless postmortem with at least one automated prevention guardrail (e.g., canary check, rate limit) before resuming normal deploys.

Practical checklist

  • Identify 1–3 critical user journeys per service (e.g., login, checkout, search) rather than tracking every internal route.
  • Express every SLI as good_events / valid_events excluding 4xx client errors and health-check noise.
  • Set internal SLOs strictly tighter than external SLAs (e.g., 99.95% SLO for a 99.9% SLA).
  • Implement multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts using Sloth or Pyrra in Prometheus (1h/5m at 14.4x, 6h/30m at 6x).
  • Add a minimum traffic threshold on burn-rate alerts so low-volume overnight periods don't trigger false pages.
  • Publish a written Error Budget Policy defining exact deploy-freeze triggers and executive override rules.
03 / Engineering trade-offs

Weighing architectural decisions

Target Tightness (99.9% vs. 99.99%)

Advantage: Each additional 'nine' cuts allowed monthly downtime by 10x (from 43.8 min to 4.38 min), satisfying strict enterprise workloads.

Drawback: Requires multi-region active-active replication, automated failover, and conservative release freezes that can double infrastructure and ops costs.

Rolling vs. Calendar Windows

Advantage: A 30-day rolling window smoothly ages out old incidents and keeps teams accountable every day rather than resetting magically on the 1st.

Drawback: Calendar windows (1st–31st) are harder to game-proof operationally, though they align better with monthly customer billing and SLA credit cycles.

Request-Based vs. Time-Based SLIs

Advantage: Request-based SLIs (good requests / total requests) weight peak-hour outages accurately when 10x more users are affected.

Drawback: Time-based SLIs (good minutes / total minutes) are simpler to compute for low-traffic services where request counts fluctuate wildly.

04 / Failure modes & resilience

Explain where it breaks · Known failure modes

Alert Fatigue from Static Thresholds

Paging on-call whenever 5xx > 0.1% for 5 minutes fires during minor background noise that would take days to exhaust the budget.

What helps: Use Google SRE multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts (e.g., page only if 1h burn >= 14.4x AND 5m burn >= 14.4x, consuming 2% of the 30-day budget in 1 hour).

Vanity SLOs (100% or Unmeasured Dependencies)

Setting a 100% target or measuring only load-balancer 200 OKs while ignoring 12-second p99 timeouts masks real user pain.

What helps: Define composite SLIs combining availability (non-5xx) AND latency (under threshold) measured as close to the client or API gateway as possible.

Low-Traffic Denominator Distortion

At 2 requests/minute overnight, 3 failed requests create a 25% error rate for 5 minutes and trigger false-positive error budget pages.

What helps: Add a minimum request volume guard (e.g., `sum(rate(http_requests_total[1h])) > 10`) or pad with synthetic blackbox probes.

Toothless Error Budget Policy

Teams track SLO dashboards in Grafana but override deployment freezes every sprint to hit product deadlines.

What helps: Get executive sign-off on a binding error budget policy—automate CI/CD deploy blocks when remaining budget drops below 0% unless an incident-fix flag is set.

05 / Practical checklist

Avoid these mistakes

  • Copying an external SLA (99.9%) directly as the internal SLO with zero safety margin.
  • Counting client-side 4xx errors (400 Bad Request, 404 Not Found) as server failures in the availability SLI.
  • Averaging latency across endpoints instead of using histogram percentiles (p95/p99) per critical user journey.
  • Forgetting that downstream dependencies cap your maximum theoretical SLO (three serial 99.9% services yield at best 99.7%).
  • Treating error budgets as a target to stay far away from rather than a resource to spend on controlled chaos testing and faster shipping.
