Capacity Planning
& Forecasting.

THE BIG IDEA / Key takeaway · CONCEPT GUIDE

Never size production from average CPU alone: model tail-latency saturation with Little's Law and the Universal Scalability Law, validate headroom with production-shape load tests, and maintain N+2 failure resilience before peak events.

Explanation anchor
01 / The idea

Foundational mechanics & core principles

Cloud autoscalers react to lagging metrics like 60-second CPU averages, which means they cannot save a system from a 10x flash-sale spike when container image pulls, JVM warmup, or RDS read-replica provisioning take 3 to 12 minutes. Without proactive capacity forecasting, teams either over-provision by 40–60% year-round or hit hard quotas (AWS EC2 vCPU limits, NAT Gateway port exhaustion, RDS max_connections) at the exact moment revenue peaks.

  • Little's Law (L = lambda * W) connects arrival rate (RPS), average residence time (latency), and required concurrency (threads, DB connections, or pods)—if latency doubles during a DB slowdown, required concurrency doubles even at constant RPS.
  • The Universal Scalability Law (USL) models throughput C(N) = N / (1 + alpha*(N-1) + betaN(N-1)), capturing both contention (alpha, e.g., lock/queue waits) and coherency delay (beta, e.g., distributed cache invalidation) that cause throughput to turn negative past an optimal node count.
  • Effective capacity must be measured at the P99 latency SLO knee—typically 60–70% CPU or 75% connection-pool utilization—not at 100% resource exhaustion where queuing delay approaches infinity.
  • Demand forecasting separates baseline trend, weekly/annual seasonality (using Holt-Winters or Prophet), and step-function marketing events, outputting P50 and P95 demand bands rather than a single point estimate.
  • Quota and dependency audits are as critical as pod counts: a 500-pod Kubernetes scale-out fails if the VPC subnet runs out of /24 IP addresses, AWS account vCPU limits cap out, or the downstream payment gateway enforces a 1,000 RPS rate limit.

Good candidates

  • Before predictable high-traffic events (Black Friday, IPL finals, tax deadlines, major product launches) where traffic jumps 3x–20x faster than reactive autoscalers can provision.
  • During quarterly FinOps and cloud reservation cycles to commit 1-year or 3-year Savings Plans and Reserved Instances against a statistically grounded baseline.
  • When onboarding new enterprise tenants or launching data-intensive features (e.g., AI inference, video transcoding) that shift the bottleneck from CPU to GPU VRAM, disk IOPS, or egress bandwidth.

Think twice

  • For early-stage prototypes or internal tools with <50 RPS where a single vertically scaled instance or serverless function handles 10x growth with negligible cost.
  • As a substitute for fixing glaring O(N^2) queries, unindexed table scans, or missing caches—throwing hardware at an unoptimized bottleneck scales cost linearly while latency still degrades.
  • When workloads are purely event-driven and bursty with sub-second cold-start tolerance (e.g., async webhook processors on AWS Lambda or Cloud Run) where pay-per-invocation eliminates idle reservation risk.

02 / Concrete walkthrough

Architectural mechanics & implementation

ScenarioAn Indian retail platform expects peak checkout traffic of 12,000 RPS (up from a 1,800 RPS baseline) during a 10-minute midnight sale. Load testing on an 8-vCPU Go service shows P99 latency stays under 45 ms up to 420 RPS per pod (70% CPU), after which GC and lock contention push P99 past 300 ms.

The idea

Capacity planning is the engineering discipline of ensuring every tier of a distributed system—compute, memory, storage IOPS, network bandwidth, and third-party API quotas—can absorb both expected demand and sudden failure-induced load shifts within strict latency SLOs.

At its core, capacity planning bridges two worlds: demand forecasting (how much work will arrive, and when?) and supply modeling (how much work can one unit of infrastructure process before tail latency violates its SLO?). Unlike naive monitoring that alerts after CPU hits 90%, rigorous capacity planning identifies the knee of the latency curve—the exact utilization point where queuing theory dictates wait times will explode—and provisions headroom ahead of time.

Concrete walkthrough

Consider sizing an order-processing microservice for a 24-hour flash sale expecting 15,000 RPS at peak:

  1. Establish the demand curve: Decompose 12 months of ingress NGINX/Envoy metrics using Prophet to separate organic growth (+6%/month) from hourly seasonality, then layer the marketing team's +400% launch spike to get a P95 peak forecast of 15,000 RPS.
  2. Find the per-unit saturation knee: Run a stepped load test (e.g., k6 or Gatling) on a single c6i.2xlarge pod (8 vCPU, 16 GB RAM). You observe P99 latency holds at 38 ms up to 480 RPS (68% CPU), then spikes to 290 ms at 560 RPS due to Go scheduler preemption and downstream DB pool waits.
  3. Apply Little's Law (L = lambda * W): At 480 RPS and 38 ms average latency, each pod holds 480 * 0.038 = 18.2 concurrent in-flight requests. If a downstream payment slow-down stretches latency to 190 ms (5x), concurrency jumps to 91 requests per pod—meaning thread/goroutine pools and DB connections must be sized for degraded latency, not just happy-path latency.
  4. Calculate N+2 zonal headroom: Baseline pods needed = ceil(15,000 / 480) = 32 pods. Across 3 Availability Zones, surviving a full AZ outage requires the remaining 2 AZs to carry all 32 pods' worth of load (16 pods/AZ, total 48 pods across 3 AZs), plus a 15% safety margin for hot-key skew -> 56 pre-warmed pods.
  5. Audit hard quotas: Verify AWS EC2 vCPU limits (56 * 8 = 448 vCPUs), VPC /22 subnet IPs, RDS max_connections behind PgBouncer, and Stripe/Razorpay API rate limits.

Closed-loop capacity planning combines time-series demand forecasting with empirical saturation profiling to size compute, storage, and cloud quotas ahead of peak events.

Queuing theory & scalability math

Two mathematical models anchor serious capacity engineering:

  • Little's Law (L = lambda * W): L is average concurrency in the system, lambda is arrival rate (requests/sec), and W is average response time (seconds). When a database lock doubles W from 50 ms to 100 ms, L doubles immediately—exhausting fixed-size worker pools (like Tomcat's 200-thread default or a 100-connection HikariCP pool) even though ingress RPS (lambda) hasn't changed.
  • Kingman's Formula (M/G/1 approximation): Wait time in a queue scales with rho / (1 - rho), where rho is utilization. Moving from 50% to 75% utilization triples queuing delay; moving from 80% to 95% multiplies it by nearly 5x. This explains why 70% is the classic safe saturation ceiling.
  • Universal Scalability Law (USL): C(N) = N / (1 + alpha*(N-1) + beta*N*(N-1)). Here alpha represents serialization/contention (mutexes, single-writer DBs) and beta represents coherency/crosstalk (Raft consensus, distributed cache invalidation). Because beta scales quadratically (N*(N-1)), adding nodes past the USL peak actually reduces total throughput.

Failure modes

  • Thundering Herd on Cold Caches: Pre-scaling stateless pods without warming Redis or CDN caches sends 100% of initial peak traffic straight to the origin database.
  • Ephemeral Port & Conntrack Exhaustion: High-RPS microservices talking to Redis or DynamoDB without HTTP keep-alive exhaust Linux ip_local_port_range (28,232 ports) or nf_conntrack_max long before CPU hits 50%.
  • Autoscaler Thrashing: Scaling up on 1-minute CPU spikes and scaling down aggressively after 3 minutes causes constant pod churn, dropping in-flight connections and invalidating local in-memory caches.
  • Silent Third-Party Quotas: Internal clusters scale cleanly, only to hit a 500 RPS hard cap on an external KYC, SMS, or payment provider.

FinOps alignment & headroom economics

Capacity planning is incomplete without unit economics. Mature teams track cost per million requests or cost per active order alongside P99 latency. Use a three-tier commitment model:

  1. Baseload (P10 floor): Cover 100% with 1-year or 3-year Compute Savings Plans / Reserved Instances (40–60% discount).
  2. Predictable Diurnal Peak: Cover with 1-year Convertible RIs or scheduled Spot fleets with On-Demand fallback.
  3. Emergency Headroom: Keep N+2 failover capacity either as pre-warmed Spot/On-Demand pools or by running low-priority batch/ML jobs on spare capacity that can be preempted via Kubernetes PriorityClass within 5 seconds of a production surge.

Practical checklist

  • Measure per-pod RPS ceiling at your exact P99 latency SLO (not 100% CPU) using production-like Zipfian key distributions.
  • Verify L = lambda * W concurrency bounds for every thread pool, HikariCP/PgBouncer pool, and Linux nf_conntrack table under 3x degraded downstream latency.
  • Enforce N+2 or >=34% spare headroom across 3 Availability Zones so losing an entire AZ does not push survivors past 80% utilization.
  • Audit cloud service quotas (vCPUs, EBS IOPS, VPC subnet IPs, NAT Gateway ports) and third-party API rate limits 14 days before peak events.
  • Pre-warm caches, pre-scale stateful databases/queues, and validate priority-based load shedding (e.g., shedding non-critical recommendations before checkout) in a game-day test.
03 / Engineering trade-offs

Weighing architectural decisions

Headroom Buffer Size (N+1 vs. N+2)

Advantage: Holding 35–50% spare capacity absorbs sudden viral spikes and full Availability Zone failures without dropping requests or triggering noisy-neighbor throttling.

Drawback: Increases baseline cloud spend by 30–50% unless offset by Spot instances, Savings Plans, or non-prod workload bin-packing on the reserved slack.

Pre-Provisioning vs. Reactive Autoscaling (HPA/Karpenter)

Advantage: Scheduled pre-scaling eliminates cold-start latency, JVM JIT warmup penalties, and EC2/EBS provisioning delays during sharp step-function traffic surges.

Drawback: Requires accurate event calendars and manual or cron-driven schedule overrides; over-predicting a marketing campaign leaves expensive idle clusters running.

Production Load Testing vs. Synthetic Modeling

Advantage: Shadow-traffic replay or controlled production load tests expose real-world bottlenecks like DNS lookup limits, NAT port exhaustion, and connection-pool deadlocks.

Drawback: Expensive to execute, risks impacting live users if blast radius controls fail, and requires synthetic data scrubbing to avoid polluting analytics or billing.

04 / Failure modes & resilience

Explain where it breaks · Known failure modes

Average-Utilization Blindness

Sizing clusters around 5-minute or 1-hour average CPU hides sub-second micro-bursts (e.g., cron storms at `:00` or mobile app retry waves) that saturate thread pools and cause 503 timeouts.

What helps: Collect 1-second or 10-second peak histograms, size for P99 burst windows, and add jittered exponential backoff on all scheduled jobs and client retries.

Hidden Quota & IP Exhaustion

Application pods have plenty of CPU headroom, but scale-out halts abruptly due to AWS EC2 vCPU service quotas, VPC subnet IP exhaustion (`InsufficientFreeAddressesInSubnet`), or NAT Gateway 55,000-port ephemeral limits.

What helps: Automate quota-utilization alerts at 70% threshold via AWS Service Quotas / Prometheus exporters, use IPv6 or secondary CIDR blocks, and enforce NAT Gateway horizontal sharding per AZ.

Non-Linear Dependency Bottleneck

Stateless API servers scale smoothly to 200 pods, but each pod opens 20 DB connections (4,000 total), crashing the primary PostgreSQL instance via context-switching and lock contention.

What helps: Model end-to-end dependency fan-out, place PgBouncer or RDS Proxy in transaction-pooling mode in front of relational databases, and cap per-pod connection pools.

Zonal Failure Cascade (Insufficient N+1/N+2)

A cluster runs at 75% average utilization across 3 Availability Zones; when 1 AZ goes dark, the remaining 2 AZs instantly jump to 112.5% load and collapse under OOMKills.

What helps: Enforce a hard ceiling of 66% utilization per AZ for 3-AZ deployments (so losing 1 AZ leaves survivors at <=99%, ideally <=80% with graceful load shedding).

05 / Practical checklist

Avoid these mistakes

  • Forecasting only compute (CPU/RAM) while ignoring disk IOPS, EBS throughput, network egress, and managed service rate limits (e.g., DynamoDB WCU/RCU or KMS API TPS).
  • Using linear extrapolation on historical averages without accounting for seasonal peaks, marketing calendar events, or the Universal Scalability Law coherency penalty.
  • Running load tests with uniform synthetic payloads and warm caches that bypass real-world hot-key skew (Zipfian distributions) and cold-cache database stampedes.
  • Forgetting to test graceful degradation—assuming capacity plans never fail instead of configuring priority-based load shedding and circuit breakers at the edge.
  • Treating capacity planning as a one-off annual spreadsheet exercise rather than a continuous weekly loop reconciling actual Prometheus/Datadog saturation against forecast bands.
